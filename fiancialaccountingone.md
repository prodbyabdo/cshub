Comprehensive Glossary of Accounting Definitions
Drawing from the sources, the following definitions are essential for understanding financial accounting principles from Chapters 1 through 6:
Accounting: The process of identifying, measuring, and communicating economic information to permit informed judgements and decisions by users of that information.
Accounting Equation: The foundation of financial accounting, showing the relationship where Assets = Capital + Liabilities.
Assets: The actual resources owned by a business, such as buildings, machinery, inventory (stocks of goods), motor vehicles, bank balances, and debts owed by customers.
Non-current Assets: Assets acquired for long-term use to provide a service to the business and not held for resale in the normal course of trading.
Current Assets: Assets likely to change in the short term (within 12 months), such as inventory, accounts receivable, and cash.
Capital: The amount of resources supplied by the owner of the business, often referred to as owner's equity or net worth.
Liabilities: Amounts owing to people other than the owner for assets supplied or funds borrowed by the business.
Current Liabilities: Debts that must be paid within one year from the date of the statement of financial position, such as accounts payable.
Profit: The amount by which revenues are greater than the cost for a set of transactions. Profit increases the business's capital.
Loss: The result when expenses exceed revenues for a set of transactions. A loss reduces the capital.
Revenues: The sales value of goods and services supplied to customers.
Cost: The total monetary amount of all the possessions (assets and expenses) used up in obtaining revenues.
Gross Profit: The excess of sales revenue over the cost of goods sold.
Net Profit: The figure remaining after all other expenses have been deducted from gross profit.
Bookkeeping: The specific part of accounting concerned with recording data relating to accounting transactions in the accounting books.
Accounts Receivable (Debtors): People or organisations who owe money to the business.
Accounts Payable (Creditors): People or organisations to whom the business owes money.
Statement of Financial Position: A report (formerly called a balance sheet) listing assets, capital, and liabilities to depict the financial situation of an organisation on a specific date.
Trial Balance: A list of account balances prepared to check that the total of all debit entries equals the total of all credit entries.
Drawings: Resources, such as cash or goods, taken out of the business by the owner for private use.
Expenses: Possessions that are immediately used up in the process of generating revenue.
Inventory Movements:
Purchases: In accounting, this refers only to goods bought with the sole intention of resale.
Sales: The sale of goods in which the business normally deals and which were bought for resale.
Returns Inwards (Sales Returns): Goods returned to the business by customers.
Returns Outwards (Purchases Returns): Goods returned by the business to their suppliers.
Carriage: Carriage Inwards are transport costs on goods purchased (added to the cost of purchases), while Carriage Outwards are transport costs for goods sent to customers.
Double Entry Terms:
Debit: An entry placed on the left side of an account.
Credit: An entry placed on the right side of an account.
Balance c/d (Carried Down): The closing balance at the end of an accounting period.
Balance b/d (Brought Down): The opening balance for the start of a new period.

Deep Dive: The MCQ "Cheat Code" (Core Logic)
To pass multiple-choice tests on Chapters 1–6, you must master the Principles of Double Entry. Every transaction consists of two elements: the Item Exchanged and the Form of Settlement.
1. The Master Axioms
The sources provide two axioms that never fail to identify the correct entry:
Axiom 1 (The Possession Rule): Any increase in a Possession (Assets and Expenses) is always a DEBIT.
Axiom 2 (The Source/Capital Rule): Any increase in Capital or Liabilities (anything not belonging to the business) is always a CREDIT.
Cheat Tip: If the circumstance is the opposite of an axiom (e.g., a decrease in a possession), you make the opposite entry (a credit).
2. Decoding "Exam Speak"
"On Time": In O Level exams, "on time" means a Credit Transaction where no cash is exchanged immediately.
If you buy "on time," you debit Purchases and credit the Supplier's (Account Payable) account.
If you sell "on time," you credit Sales and debit the Customer's (Account Receivable) account.
Immediate Settlement: If a transaction is paid by cheque or cash immediately, never enter it in a supplier's or customer's personal account; instead, use the Bank or Cash account.
3. Inventory Logic (The Four Accounts)
Because inventory is usually sold at a profit, you cannot use one single "Inventory Account" for daily transactions. You must use:
Purchases (Debit): When you buy goods for resale.
Sales (Credit): When you sell goods to customers.
Returns Inwards (Debit): When customers return goods to you (inventory increases).
Returns Outwards (Credit): When you return goods to suppliers (inventory decreases).
4. The Trial Balance "Safety Check"
Always Debit: Assets, Expenses, Drawings, Losses, and Returns Inwards.
Always Credit: Liabilities, Capital, Revenues, Profits, and Returns Outwards.
Limitations: A Trial Balance only proves that Total Debits = Total Credits. It does not find all errors, such as completely omitting a transaction.
5. Balancing-Off Mechanics
Debit Balance: If the left side is larger than the right, the account has a debit balance (typical for Assets/Expenses).
Credit Balance: If the right side is larger, it is a credit balance (typical for Liabilities/Capital).
The c/d and b/d Rule: The balance c/d is entered on the smaller side to make the totals equal at the end of the month; the balance b/d is then brought down to the opposite (and correct) side to start the next month.
To handle the "t-shaped baby" (the T-account) and manage the c/d (carried down) and b/d (brought down) entries correctly, you need to follow the master logic of double entry.
1. Which Side to Open (The Opening Balance)
To "open" an account at the start of a period, you use a Balance b/d (brought down). The side you put it on depends on whether the item is a possession or a source of funds:
Debit Side (Left): Use this for Assets (e.g., Cash, Bank, Inventory, Debtors/Accounts Receivable) and Expenses.
Credit Side (Right): Use this for Liabilities (e.g., Loans, Creditors/Accounts Payable) and Capital.
2. The Mechanics of "c/d" and "b/d"
Balancing-off an account is the process of finding the difference between the two sides at the end of the month. Follow these five stages:
Total Both Sides: Add up the debits and credits in your head or on scrap paper to see which side is bigger.
Calculate the Difference: Subtract the smaller total from the larger total. This is your "balance".
Insert "Balance c/d": Write the difference on the smaller side and label it "Balance c/d." This makes both sides mathematically equal so you can close the month.
Rule Off: Draw a single line above the totals and a double line underneath. The totals on both the left and right must be exactly level with each other.
Bring it Down ("b/d"): Now, move that same amount to the opposite side below the double lines. Label it "Balance b/d" with the date of the first day of the next month.

Answer Sheet: Step-by-Step Logic
To solve these, you must identify what belongs to the business (Assets) and what the business owes (Liabilities). The difference is Capital (Capital = Assets - Liabilities).
Solution for 1.7 (B. Wise)
Identify Assets: Van (£4,500) + Stall (£2,000) + Inventory (£1,500) + Cash in Hand (£400) + Bank (£1,100) = Total Assets: £9,500.
Identify Liabilities: Amount owed for stock (£1,000) + Loan from C. Fox (£5,000) = Total Liabilities: £6,000.
Apply Equation: £9,500 (Assets) - £6,000 (Liabilities) = £3,500 Capital.
Solution for 1.8A (F. Flint)
Identify Assets: Fixtures (£1,200) + Van (£6,000) + Inventory (£2,800) + Bank (£200) + Cash in Hand (£175) = Total Assets: £10,375.
Identify Liabilities: Account Payable (£1,600) + Loan from B. Rub (£2,500) = Total Liabilities: £4,100.
Apply Equation: £10,375 (Assets) - £4,100 (Liabilities) = £6,275 Capital.
Solution for 1.9 (G. Stoner)
Identify Assets: Car (£16,600) + Equipment (£15,800) + Inventory (£11,400) + Accounts Receivable (£1,600) + Bank (£800) = Total Assets: £46,200.
Identify Liabilities: Accounts Payable = £1,800.
Apply Equation: £46,200 (Assets) - £1,800 (Liabilities) = £44,400 Capital.
Cheat Note for CS Students: In a Statement of Financial Position, always present Non-current Assets (long-term use) first, followed by Current Assets (short-term), then deduct Current Liabilities to show Net Assets, which will equal your Capital.
